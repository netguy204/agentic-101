# Before Session 1

<!-- Chunk: docs/chunks/fluency_session_01 - Session 1 prework -->

**About 25 minutes.** Three things: something to read, something to jot
down, and one line to send your facilitator.

Nothing here needs a terminal, and nothing here needs the software you
installed before the course. Session 1 is a conversation. If your setup
didn't work, part 3 tells you what to do about it — and you will not miss
anything in Session 1 because of it.

---

## 1. Read this (10 minutes)

### Why this course, why you

You already know your job deeply — the edge cases, the tedious reports,
the workarounds you've built in spreadsheets, the reason the numbers in
that one column are always wrong in March. That knowledge is about to
become a new kind of leverage.

Here's the argument, and it's worth reading slowly, because everything
else on this course follows from it.

For about forty years, one profession has had a structural advantage that
had nothing to do with being smarter than anyone else. A software engineer
could take a decision — *when this happens, do that; this input is
suspicious; this case needs a human* — write it down once in code, and
have it govern thousands of executions afterwards, without them in the
room. Their judgment compounded.

Everyone else's judgment ran live. Yours has run one meeting at a time,
one review at a time, one document at a time. You have made thousands of
good calls, and each of them applied exactly once. Not because your
judgment was worth less — because there was no way to encode it that
didn't require learning to program, and learning to program was a career,
not an afternoon.

That's what's changed. Agentic tools let judgment expressed in **plain
language** govern automated work. The instruction "flag anything from a
customer who's mentioned cancelling, because retention is what we're
protecting this quarter" is now a thing you can write down once and have
applied every time, by something that does the typing for you. No code, no
ticket to IT, no waiting.

Which means the scarce input has moved. It used to be the keystrokes —
someone who could build the thing. Now the keystrokes are cheap and the
scarce input is knowing **what's worth building and whether the result is
right**. That is exactly what you have and exactly what these tools don't.

So this course is not about catching up to programmers. It's not about
learning to code — you won't write any. It's about the thing that's now
in short supply: your judgment, and how to spend it where it buys the
most.

### The catch, stated honestly

There's a hard edge to this, and you'll meet it in the session.

You can safely hand over work you couldn't do yourself. That's normal —
you do it every time you hire a contractor or brief a designer. What you
cannot safely hand over is work whose result **you couldn't evaluate**.
An agent will produce a confident, fluent, professional-looking answer to
almost anything you ask. If you're not able to tell a good one from a bad
one, you will not find out that it was wrong. You'll find out later, from
someone else, expensively.

Being able to *do* the work and being able to *judge* the work are two
different abilities, and only one of them is the limit. Session 1 is about
finding where yours runs out — and that turns out to be a genuinely useful
thing to know about yourself, quite apart from any of this.

## 2. Jot down five things (10 minutes)

Think back over your **last working week** — not a typical week, the
actual one. Write down **five things you did more than once**, or that you
do every week, or that you'd do again next week without anyone asking.

Rough is fine. Scribbled on the back of something is fine. Nobody is going
to read this, and you'll rewrite it properly in the session. Aim for the
tedious end of your job rather than the impressive end.

Prompts, if the page stays blank:

- What did you do on Monday morning before you got to anything real?
- What do you copy from one place into another place?
- What do you chase people for?
- What do you reformat?
- What do you check, every time, in case someone got it wrong?
- What's the thing you'd hand to a new joiner first, because it's
  tedious and low-risk?

Bring the list. Paper, phone, laptop — whatever you'll actually have with
you.

> **If your work is confidential** and you'd rather not discuss it, even in
> outline: come anyway and say so. There's a prepared alternative set of
> tasks you can use in the exercises, and it works just as well. Let your
> facilitator know in part 3 so they have it ready for you.

---

## 3. Tell your facilitator two things (2 minutes)

Reply to your facilitator's email with:

1. **Your setup status.** Before the course you were sent a setup guide
   that ends with a check saying `PASS` or `FAIL`. Which did you get? If
   you didn't get through the guide at all, say that — it's a common
   answer and it's fine.
2. **Whether you'll be using your own work in the exercises**, or would
   prefer the prepared alternative.

**If your setup failed, or you never finished it: stop there. Don't try to
fix it.** Do not reinstall anything, do not follow instructions from
elsewhere on the internet, and do not lose an evening to it. Session 1
doesn't use the software at all — you'll take part in every minute of it
either way. Your facilitator will book you a short slot to fix it together
before Session 2, which is the session that actually needs it. Arriving
with a broken setup costs you nothing; arriving having spent three hours
fighting it costs you an evening you won't get back.

---

## What to bring

- Your list of five things.
- Something to write with, and something to write on. Paper is
  recommended — Session 1 is mostly thinking and talking, and a laptop
  between you and the room doesn't help.
- No software, no terminal, no preparation beyond this page.
