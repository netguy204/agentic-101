# Session 3 — Anatomy of a delegation

This is the concept track for session three, and it is the session built on
the one that went badly. By now you have handed an agent work three or four
times, and at least one of those did not go the way you wanted. That is the
material. Nothing here asks you to type anything; the steps, the sheet and
the commands are all waiting for you at the desk. What is here is the part
worth having in your head first: why bad delegations go bad, the four things
a delegation needs, and the one move to make when it goes wrong. Four
segments, about four or five minutes each, and each one closes with a
question to answer out loud.

## Segment 1 — There are only three

Let me start with the claim this whole session rests on, because if it is
true it is worth a great deal, and it sounds too tidy to be true.

Nearly every bad delegation is one of three things. Not thirty. Three.

The first is that it did not know something. It needed a file, a fact, a
date, a rule — and that thing was not there, so it guessed. This is the one
people underestimate, because agents guess politely. They do not stop and
say "I am missing a crucial piece of information here". They pick something
reasonable and carry on in a confident tone, and quite often they do not
mention it at all. Somebody tells it to chase up the stale items and never
says what stale means, and it decides stale means ten days, and it chases
four people who were perfectly fine.

The second is that it decided something. It made a choice you had not asked
it to make. It merged two labels that mean different things where you work.
It picked a date format. It filled in a blank. In session two, most of a
room watched an agent fold "Complete" into "Done" — which is either
completely harmless or a small disaster, depending entirely on facts about
your organisation that were never written down anywhere it could see.

The third is the strangest one and it is the one this course cares about
most. I could not tell. It might have been fine. It might have been perfect.
You had no way of knowing, because there was nothing you could actually look
at and check. Nothing failed. You just could not see.

That is the list. Three reasons.

Now, why does that matter? Because each of those three is a missing piece of
information, and information is a thing you can supply in advance. It did
not know something means you did not tell it. It decided something means you
did not say which way. I could not tell means you never worked out what
you would look at. None of those three are failures of the software, and
that is the reframe I want you to arrive with. The instinct, when a
delegation goes badly, is to conclude something about the tool — it is not
very good, or it is not there yet, or it is fine for other people's work but
not for mine. That conclusion is comfortable and it teaches you nothing,
because there is no next step after it.

The other conclusion — the thing I did not tell it was this specific thing —
has a next step, and the next step takes about ninety seconds.

The first exercise of this session is where you find out whether the claim
holds. Everybody has brought one that went badly, and you say what happened
in one sentence, and you sort it into one of the three. If you are doing
this on your own, you sort your own, in writing, and you should still expect
the sorting to be surprisingly easy. If you are in a room, you get to watch
a dozen different people's disasters land in three buckets, which is the
fastest way to believe it.

One important extra. There is a fourth answer, and it is not a failure of
the sorting. Sometimes the honest answer is that the task was outside what
you could judge, and you noticed. Out of bounds. That counts as a pass, and
it will come back at the end of this track as the most valuable thing that
can happen in this session.

Say your sentence plainly, by the way. What you asked for, what went wrong.
Do not explain it and do not defend it. The point of the sentence is not
to be a good account of yourself; it is to be raw enough to sort.

## Segment 2 — A colleague who cannot see your screen

So if there are three ways it goes wrong, and each one is a missing piece of
information, the obvious question is: what is the complete list of things an
agent needs? What do you owe it before you hand something over?

Here is the frame that makes the answer easy to remember, and it is the one
this session uses throughout. Imagine briefing a capable colleague who
cannot see your screen, does not know your company, and will not ask.

Sit with each part of that for a second, because all three are doing work.
Capable — they are good at the job, and you are not writing instructions for
an idiot. Cannot see your screen — they have no idea which file, which
folder, what is on it. Does not know your company — every convention you
have absorbed over four years is invisible to them. And will not ask. That
last one is the sharp one. A real colleague stops and says "sorry, when you
say stale, what do you mean?" This one does not. It makes its best guess and
keeps going, and its best guess is exactly as confident as the rest of it.

So what do you owe that person? Four things.

What they must be able to see. The files, and the facts. Not just the files
— the facts too, the things that are not written down anywhere, the ones you
know because you have been there four years. That "Done" and "Complete" mean
different things. That the deadline is Monday. That the figures for one team
always arrive late and that is normal.

What done right looks like. And here is the thing: you have already written
this. It is column two of your inventory sheet, from session one, from
before you had ever run any of this software. What the session asks you to
do is sharpen it into one sentence something can act on.

What done produces, and where. A file name. A place. A shape. If you cannot
name the thing that is supposed to exist at the end, you are going to have
trouble deciding whether it does.

And where you will look to check, and what for. This is column four of your
inventory, turned into a step you will actually take. Not "I would read it
over". What are you reading it for?

Four things. Inputs, instruction, outputs, checkpoints. Written on one page,
that page is called a delegation brief, and filling one in is the second
exercise of this session.

Two of those four you have owned since session one. The other two —
inputs and outputs — are, in my experience, exactly the two that were
missing when it went wrong. Look back at the three reasons. It did not know
something is a missing input. It decided something is a missing input or a
loose instruction. I could not tell is a checkpoint you never wrote.

One warning about the brief, because it is the commonest way to write a bad
one. Under pressure, people write briefs to be defensible rather than
useful. Vague enough that nothing in them can be wrong. A brief like that
cannot fail — and because it cannot fail, running it teaches you nothing,
because there is nothing specific for the run to disagree with. Be literal.
Name the file. Say what must not be touched. If the file does not exist yet,
describe its shape, because you will make a pretend version of it in the
next exercise anyway.

## Segment 3 — Find the box, change it once, run again

Now the centre of the session, which is a loop you have already run once and
are about to run twice on purpose.

You know the shape from session two. Save a snapshot of the folder, hand the
work over, read what it did, check it, save again. This session runs that
twice, with one change to the brief in between, and almost everything
interesting is in the phrase "one change".

Here is what happens. You run the brief the first time. You read it the way
session two taught you: the boxes it shows you, then the actual file, then
your own checkpoint, applied literally — open the thing you said you would
open, look for the thing you said you would look for. And then you ask four
questions, one per box. Did it have what it needed, or did it guess? Did it
do what I meant, or what I said? Is the result where I said, shaped how I
said? And did my check catch anything — and could it have?

One of those four will have a bad answer. That is the box.

Then you change that one box, and you write down why, in one sentence, at
the bottom of the sheet. And here is the discipline: one box, one change. If
two things went wrong, fix the bigger one. The second run will tell you
about the other.

That rule irritates people, so let me defend it. If you change three things
and the second run is better, you have learned that some combination of
three changes helped, which is nearly worthless, because next week you will
not know which of them to bother with. If you change one thing and the run
is better, you know something true about your own work that you can use
forever. A change and a run costs two minutes. Three changes and a guess
costs you a week of not knowing.

Then there is one mechanical move, and it is the only genuinely new
mechanism in this session, so I want to give you the reason rather than the
keystrokes. Before the second run, you put the folder back to where it was
before the first run. Not to the finished state — to the starting line. The
reason is that otherwise you are running the revised brief on material the
first run already worked on, and you are comparing two different runs on two
different starting points, which compares nothing. Going back to the start
is what makes the second run an experiment rather than a continuation. It is
also, as you learned in session two, entirely undoable — going back is
itself recorded, so nothing is lost.

Then you run it again, read it the same way, and save it.

Two things to expect. The first is that the sentence at the bottom of your
sheet is the point of the entire session. Not the brief, not the two runs.
The sentence. "I added the fact that stale means ten days, because it
guessed" is what a pass sounds like. "I made it clearer" is not — it names
no box and no reason, and it is by a distance the commonest near-miss in
this session. If you catch yourself writing something like it, ask yourself:
clearer how? What did the first run do that the second one did not?

The second thing to expect is that your first run may simply be fine. It
happens, and it is not a problem, and there is a specific move for it: your
one change goes to the checkpoints box instead. Add the check that would
have caught the thing you were most worried about. Then run again. A second
run that is also fine, with a better check than you started with, is a pass
and always was.

## Segment 4 — The box that decides

Last segment, and it is the one the session exists for. Everything so far
has been getting you to a place where this question is answerable.

The question is: what could go wrong with this task that my checks would not
catch?

There is a fifth box on the brief for the answer, and it is filled in last,
after the thing has run twice, because you cannot answer it honestly in
advance. You need to have watched it work to know what it is capable of
doing quietly.

Think about what you are actually being asked. Not what went wrong — you
already handled that, it is the box you changed. This is about the failure
that would have sailed straight past you. Two people's figures swapped: your
row count is identical either way. Two labels merged that mean different
things where you work. A number that is plausible and wrong. Something
missing in a way that leaves no gap where you were looking.

The second of the three reasons is where most of these live. The decisions
it makes that you did not ask for are, almost by definition, the ones your
checks are not looking for, because you did not know they were going to
happen.

If nothing comes to mind, use the question that always works. What would you
be most embarrassed to have missed? There is always one. That is the box.

And then the brief forces a decision, which is the real payload of this
session. You do one of two things.

Either you add a checkpoint that would catch it — a second line, as concrete
as the first, open this and look for that. Congratulations: you now have a
better brief than you walked in with, and a task that is genuinely safer to
hand over.

Or you write "out" beside the task. Out means you cannot think of a check
you could actually perform. Not a check that exists in principle — one you
would really do, on a Monday, in the time you have.

I want to be extremely clear about what "out" means here, because everything
in your instincts will read it as failure. It is the best outcome available
in this session. Think about what you have just done: you took a task, you
wrote it down properly, you ran it twice, you watched it work, and you
established — with evidence, on your own work — that it sits outside what
you can currently judge. That is session one's judgment boundary, found by
experiment rather than guessed at. And you found it for the price of two
runs in a practice folder, instead of the price of running it weekly for
real and discovering the problem in six months, in front of somebody.

The pass I would most like to hear from this session sounds like this. The
run was fine both times. My check was counting the rows, and I realised that
would never catch it swapping two people's figures. I cannot think of a
check for that I could actually do on a Monday without redoing the whole
thing. So I have written out. It was a forty-minute job and I will keep
doing it by hand.

That person is not behind. That person is doing precisely the thing this
course is for, and in the last session, when it comes to deciding what to
build, that word counts as the skill.

One thing to watch, though. Out has to be a finding and not an exit. "It is
too hard" is not out. The test is: could anyone in your role check this? If
somebody with your knowledge could, then what would they look at — and that
is your second checkpoint, not an out.

So: four boxes, two runs, one change, one sentence, and one honest answer
about what you still cannot see. That is the whole session, and the next one
asks what happens when the same brief has to run every week.
